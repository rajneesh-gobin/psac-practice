                             About the Teacher’s Book

The purpose of this Teacher’s Book is to help teachers organise pupils’ learning experiences
in SSEE in a more effective way and to enhance the understanding and appreciation of the
environment by pupils. It provides basic information on each unit for teachers. It highlights the
objectives, teaching points, concepts to focus on and helpful hints. Additional notes on subject
knowledge are provided to the teacher for some concepts whenever required. The Teacher’s
Book not being exhaustive, teachers may need to search for additional information from other
reliable sources.


It is to be noted that in keeping with the National Curriculum Framework (2015), the Social,
Scientific and Environmental Education (SSEE) curriculum “enables learners to acquire subject
knowledge and conceptual understanding, develop inquiry skills, attitudes and values for a
critical understanding of the three dimensions of the environment: natural, cultural and
social.”


The aims of SSEE in the primary curriculum are to ensure that learners:


•    Develop curiosity about the world around them
•    Develop knowledge and a critical understanding of the natural, cultural and social environment
•    Appreciate the interdependence of all living things
•    Appreciate and care for the environment
•    Develop an understanding of their cultural and historical heritage
•    Understand how to act as responsible citizens
•    Develop a sense of personal and national identity
•    Use a variety of ICT tools in exploring the environment


Effective Pedagogy


A successful implementation of the SSEE curriculum will depend largely on the way it is taught
to pupils. The pedagogical plan for the units in the Pupil’s Book should consist of different
types of teaching/learning and assessment strategies. Teachers should consider the following,
without, however, limiting themselves to them:


Cooperative (Peer) Learning


It is important for pupils to work together to gain and share knowledge and understand
the world around them. ‘Discuss and Share’ is a component of the book that allows pupils to
work with their peers (in pairs or in groups) to learn and share. This component of the book gives
suggestions for classroom discussions, helps pupils to share and work with others and in the

iv
process promotes peer learning, develops oral communication skills and reinforces concepts.
Brainstorming, concept mapping, debates, games, visits to places of interests are other means
of learning cooperatively.


Learning and Teaching by Inquiry


Inquiry is a method of teaching and learning that actively involves the learner and in the process
leads him/her to understand concepts.


Passive teaching strategies like ‘chalk and talk’ will not meet and promote the objectives of the
SSEE curriculum. To achieve the aims listed above, SSEE should be taught through active and
child-centred teaching/learning strategies. Pupils should be encouraged to learn by doing and
discovery. It is recommended that pupils be involved in all activities which include the inquiry
skills mentioned in the SSEE curriculum. The Pupil’s Book for Grade 3 focusses on observation,
comparison, classification, data collection/recording and the communication of findings and
ideas.


Pupils should be encouraged to manipulate materials (photos, maps, pictures, items), specimens
and simple equipment safely. The set of ‘Activities’ in the book actively engages the pupils to
construct subject knowledge, explore and engage with the content and develop inquiry skills,
attitudes and values. A lot of ‘illustrations’ are used to complement the writing and to give
young learners a lot of opportunities to observe and in the process help visual learners also.


Motivating Pupils and Arousing their Interests


The SSEE curriculum can be made more interesting to pupils by providing them with situations
and problems that are challenging, thought-provoking, stimulating and that encourage them to
reflect on phenomena relevant to their daily life experiences.


‘Did you know?’ triggers the interest and curiosity of pupils.


‘Think and Think!’ stretches the thinking of pupils and develops their creativity. Pupils should
also be encouraged to think, wherever possible, about the everyday applications of the basic
scientific principles they have learnt.




                                                                                                v
‘Find out’ prompts pupils to think and learn beyond the content of the book and underscores
the fact that the book should not be seen as the only source of knowledge. This activity is
meant to prompt pupils to develop the habit of looking for information on their own from relevant
sources. The emphasis is not only on finding the correct answer but also on properly exploring
the various sources of information available including the Internet.


The use of learning resources such as living and non-living specimens, models, pictures, maps,
drawings, photographs, press and magazine cuttings and low-cost equipment is essential to
make learning interesting for pupils.


Using ICT


The use of ICT tools to explore and discover the environment must be encouraged throughout
the book. ICT can help in broadening and deepening pupils’ learning. It is to be noted that digital
resources will be developed and made accessible to both teachers and learners.


Formative and Summative Assessment


Pupils should be assessed in many ways to determine their understanding. It is important to
note that many exercises are inbuilt into the units for formative purposes. It is recommended
that assessment and evaluation be continuous and comprehensive and aligned to how pupils
learn and are taught.


For example, asking pupils questions about prior knowledge (what they have learnt in previous
classes) will build on knowledge progressively. Speaking with pupils, asking them questions
about the activity to see what they learnt from it is a good way of assessing what a child
knows and understands from a particular activity. Creating maps and drawings is another way
to informally assess pupils in a group or individually to see what they can come up with.


‘I check my progress’ represents a checklist of attained learning outcomes that the pupils can
read, understand and use to assess their own learning.


‘What I have learnt’ summarises key learning points.




vi
‘Words to learn’ focusses on key vocabulary and promotes literacy development.
A unit is not complete without a way to assess the pupils to figure out what they gained from
their learning experiences in the classroom. At the end of each unit, pupils will be tested on
their knowledge and understanding. ‘End-of-unit exercises’ check the progress of pupils
and consolidate knowledge and understanding. More authentic performance assessment
instruments to assess what the pupils know and understand must be developed by the
teacher related to content knowledge, understanding and skills development. Pupils must
receive feedback on what they need to work on in order to improve performance and reach the
next level.


I sincerely hope that the teaching of SSEE will be effective and learning will be enjoyable to
all pupils.

Unit 1: Looking at our Environment

Pupils must learn how we understand the world around us. In this unit, pupils will use their senses
to observe and gather information from the environment around them. As pupils learn everything
through the five senses, the lessons incorporated in Unit 1 will engage pupils in activities to explore
the environment around them through their five senses. This unit will explain each of the five sense
organs and senses in detail and give pupils a chance to explore their environment using their
five senses. They will be using their eyes, skin (hands), ears, nose, and tongue to gain knowledge
of the environment around them.

The objectives of the Unit 1 are to enable the pupil to:

•   Write a simple definition of the environment
•   Ask many questions about the environment
•   Name the different parts of the body that help us observe the environment
•   Use the eyes, ears, nose, tongue and hands to observe the environment
•   Identify and name the five sense organs
•   Tell what each sense organ helps us to do
•   Name the five senses
•   Relate the five senses to how we see, hear, touch, smell and taste.

In keeping with the SSEE curriculum, pupils are given a lot of opportunities throughout the units to
develop inquiry skills, scientific attitudes and values. Pupils will be given opportunities in Unit 1 to
develop skills such as questioning, observing, classifying, comparing and communicating.

Inquiry Skills

Questioning		          - Asking questions about animals, plants, objects and events in the
			                      environment around them
Observing		            - Using the senses to gather information from the environment around them
Classifying		          - Grouping objects according to observable characteristics
Comparing		            - Identifying similarities and differences
Communicating          - Receiving and transmitting information orally, in writing or by drawing
			                      pictures, tables and models

Attitudes and Values

Cooperation		          - Sharing with others and working together with others

Curiosity		 - Asking questions about objects and events and finding out more about
			           things around them on their own

To observe means to look at something closely and carefully


Activity 1.4 Using your body to observe the environment

Objective: To make learners aware of the importance of different parts of the body to observe the
environment and introduce the sense organs.

In Grades 1 and 2, pupils learnt about the different parts of the body. In this unit, the main external
body parts associated with the sense organs are taken up, and pupils are expected to elaborate
on the main function(s) of each. Furthermore, the senses associated with different sense organs are
also mentioned. Pupils should be encouraged to learn more about their different senses through
the various activities described in this unit.

Helpful hints:
• Make a brief recapitulation of what pupils learnt in Grades 1 and 2 about the main
   body parts.
• Ask pupils to draw a human body.
• Ask pupils to write down in their exercise book the names of the different body parts.
• Have an oral discussion of the main functions of each body part by asking questions: How
   does the body part help you everyday? e.g. hands to touch, hold or carry objects; legs for
   walking and running/moving etc.

The unit begins with a story about using the five sense organs that will help to open the discussion
about using the body to observe the environment.  Read the story to the pupils to give them a
general idea of the following action verbs: see, hear, touch, smell and taste. Focus on each action
verb individually before bringing them all together.  

In relation to Activity 1.4, ask pupils

•   to read the story and to mention the sense organs
•   to study the pictures and read out the name of the sense organs shown
•   to make the link between the different sense organs and the five senses
•   to fill table on page 15 in their exercise book.

In relation to Activity 1.4, help pupils understand the following:

We have five sense organs.
We use the eyes to see.
We use the tongue to taste.
We use the nose to smell.
We use the ears to hear.
We use the skin (hands) to touch and feel.
The five sense organs help us to observe our environment.

Additional Notes to the Teacher: The Human Body

The human body is made up of many parts, each of which is suited to its function. Among the
external body organs, the sense organs are very important. Without being able to sense the
environment, it would be difficult to move and act. The five senses are sight, hearing, smell, taste
and touch. All these senses send information to the brain which then tells us what we see, hear,
smell, taste and touch (or feel).

The eyes are responsible for the sense of sight. Eyes respond to light. They can be compared to
two tiny cameras inside the head. They send a steady stream of pictures back to the brain, like a
television camera sending live pictures back to a television screen.

  Additional Notes to the Teacher: The Sense of Hearing

  The ears allow us to hear sounds. Sound is produced when an object vibrates. These vibrations
  cause the air around to vibrate as well, producing sound waves. The ear can distinguish the
  strength, speed and direction of the vibrations.

I am growing

Question to focus on:
How do I know I have grown?

Objectives: At the end of the lesson pupils will:
• Observe changes in themselves
• Talk about changes in themselves that occur over time.

Key Concepts: Time and Change

Vocabulary: Last Year, This Year, Larger, Bigger, Longer, Taller, Same

Skills: Observing changes, Discussing changes, Comparing changes

Processes: Drawing pictures, Collecting information, Completing timelines

Procedures
Emphasise on key vocabulary associated with time (for example: Last year, This Year, Now, Today).
Ask oral questions: In which Grade are you this year? In which Grade were you last year? What
are you doing now? What did you do yesterday? You could ask questions related to any activity
ensuring that the key vocabulary associated with time is used (for example: activities done last
week….)
Then gradually introduce questions about the changes that they can observe in themselves. You can
also draw their attention to the fact that some things have changed but others are still the same. Make
pupils aware that some changes are natural while others can be man made (for example, changes
in the environment, weather (natural), new buildings (man-made) and then move to changes
in them).

The focus is to enable the pupils to understand ‘change’ in relation to the passing of ‘time’.

Activity 1: Observing and recording changes in the school environment for a week.
This can be a group activity. The teacher can divide the class in groups. Each group will have to
observe at least five things that have changed in a week (activity can start on Monday and last till
Friday). Pupils can be asked to observe changes in the school yard, classroom, weather….. Pupils
can write words or even draw to show the changes. (Bristol sheet to be provided to the pupils to
record their observation which can be displayed). Class discussion to follow the activity.

Activity 2: Observing changes in themselves.
Observe and discuss different pictures of children ranging from the time they were still babies until
their present age and relate the changes to their own experience. (Teachers can refer to Activity
2.2 provided in the book to initiate discussion in the class). Discuss personal changes in size, height,
age, shape, new uniform, shoes (Discussion to include terms: tall, short, taller, bigger, smaller,
larger etc). Objects/pictures of things used when they were still a baby until now (eight years)
could also be brought by pupils in class to observe and discuss changes. Teachers may use non-
Grade measurement to record change (for example: strings or strips to measure height/size and
compare these characteristic features with the pupils in class).

Activity 3: Creative activity
Pupils can draw their own pictures to show changes in themselves (size, height). Encourage them
to talk about the pictures.

Assessment: Oral/written response to questions/Drawing/ Completing timeline

        Teacher’s note pg 114-123

  Activity 4.12 : I classify objects.

  The aim of this activity is to provide pupils with an opportunity to classify objects based on the
  materials they are made of.

  The pupils are provided with pictures of 9 objects. They must be given enough time to observe
  these pictures carefully to be able to classify them in the appropriate column in the table given
  on page 115.

  Teachers may also bring pictures of other objects or real objects made of the different materials
  and allow pupils to classify them based on the material used to make these objects. In addition,
  pupils may also be invited to bring pictures of different objects to extend the activity.

  Another important point is that pupils may also be exposed to objects which are made of more
  than one materials, for example chairs and tables made of glass and wood. These would help
  pupils to become aware of the fact that some objects are made of only one material, but others
  are made of two or more materials.

  Activities 4.11 and 4.12 have been designed to allow pupils to develop awareness of the 3 types of
  materials and to recognise objects made of these materials. This would form the basis for further
  learning about materials in higher classes where they would learn that the materials are selected
  for making objects based on their properties.

  Reinforcement: Teacher is advised to bring children in school yard for an outdoor activity and ask
  them to list other objects and find out what each object is made of.

  Enrichment: Pupils to make a list of other objects found at home and classify them based on what
  they are made of. Pupils can share and discuss their findings.
