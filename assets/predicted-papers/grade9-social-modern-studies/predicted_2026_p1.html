SECTION B — Source-Based Questions [30 marks] Answer ALL questions in this section.
SOURCE A
Mauritius has been widely recognised as one of Africa's most stable and mature democracies. Since gaining independence in 1968, the country has consistently held regular, free, and fair elections under a parliamentary system of government. The Constitution of Mauritius, which serves as the supreme law of the land, enshrines fundamental rights and freedoms including freedom of expression, freedom of conscience, the right to a fair trial, and protection from discrimination on grounds of race, caste, religion, or sex. The National Assembly consists of 62 directly elected members and up to 8 additional seats allocated through the Best Loser System to ensure that minority communities are fairly represented. The Prime Minister, as head of government, is accountable to the National Assembly, which exercises oversight through parliamentary debate, question time, and select committees. The President of Mauritius, appointed by the National Assembly, serves as a constitutional head of state with largely ceremonial functions.
Adapted from: Democracy Index, Mauritius Country Profile, 2024

SOURCE C
"Globalisation has created unprecedented opportunities for small economies to integrate into global value chains and access foreign technology, investment, and markets. Countries like Mauritius have successfully diversified their economies — from sugar monoculture to tourism, manufacturing, and financial services — largely because of their openness to the global economy. However, globalisation is not without risks. Many developing nations face challenges such as increased competition from cheaper imports that threaten local industries, greater economic vulnerability to external shocks such as global recessions and pandemics, cultural homogenisation as Western consumer habits spread, and the exodus of skilled workers seeking better opportunities abroad (known as 'brain drain'). Small island developing states (SIDS) are particularly exposed to these risks because of their limited economic size, narrow production base, and heavy dependence on a small number of trading partners. The key challenge for governments is to maximise the benefits of openness while protecting the most vulnerable sectors and workers."
Adapted from: UNDP Report on Globalisation and Small Island Developing States, 2023
