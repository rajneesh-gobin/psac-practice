SECTION A — READING COMPREHENSION   [30 marks]

Read the passage carefully, then answer ALL the questions that follow.

More Than the Absence of Illness: Rethinking Health and Wellbeing in Teenagers

For much of the twentieth century, health was understood primarily as the absence of disease. A person was considered healthy if they were not suffering from a physical illness, and the role of medicine was to treat those who fell sick. Today, however, this narrow definition has been largely replaced by a broader and more nuanced understanding: that genuine health encompasses not only physical fitness, but also mental, emotional, and social wellbeing. For teenagers in particular, this holistic view is not merely desirable — it is essential.

Adolescence is a period of remarkable transformation. The brain undergoes significant development during the teenage years, with the prefrontal cortex — the region responsible for planning, decision-making, and impulse control — not fully maturing until the mid-twenties. This makes teenagers uniquely vulnerable to stress, peer pressure, and risky behaviour, but also uniquely receptive to positive influences. The habits formed during adolescence — whether around diet, exercise, sleep, or emotional regulation — often persist well into adulthood.

Sleep is perhaps the most underestimated factor in teenage health. Research consistently shows that adolescents require between eight and ten hours of sleep per night, yet surveys indicate that the majority get far less. Chronic sleep deprivation in teenagers has been linked to poor academic performance, heightened emotional reactivity, impaired immune function, and an increased risk of obesity and depression. Despite this evidence, many schools continue to schedule lessons as early as seven-thirty in the morning — a practice that some health experts now consider harmful.

Physical activity is equally important. The World Health Organisation recommends that children and teenagers engage in at least sixty minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity each day. Regular exercise not only strengthens the heart and muscles, but also releases endorphins — the brain's natural mood-enhancing chemicals — and has been shown to reduce symptoms of anxiety and depression. Yet screen-based entertainment and academic pressure have led to a dramatic decline in the physical activity levels of young people worldwide.

Addressing teenage health effectively requires action at every level: from individual choices about diet and rest, to school policies that prioritise student wellbeing, to government investment in sports facilities, mental health services, and health education. Above all, it requires a cultural shift in the way we talk about health — moving away from quick fixes and physical appearance, and towards a genuine, sustained commitment to young people's long-term flourishing.
