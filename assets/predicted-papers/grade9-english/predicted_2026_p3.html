SECTION A — READING COMPREHENSION   [30 marks]

Read the passage carefully, then answer ALL the questions that follow.

Belonging Everywhere and Nowhere: The Question of Cultural Identity

In an age of globalisation, mass migration, and instant digital communication, the question of cultural identity has become more complex and more urgent than ever before. For millions of young people around the world — particularly those who grow up between two or more cultures — the question "Where are you really from?" is not simply a matter of geography. It touches the deepest layers of who they believe themselves to be.

Culture is not inherited in the way that eye colour or height might be. It is absorbed — through language, food, music, religious practice, storytelling, and the countless small rituals of daily life. For children who straddle two worlds, this absorption is often a process of negotiation: which elements of each culture to embrace, which to set aside, and how to reconcile values that sometimes appear to contradict one another.

Mauritius offers a compelling case study. Home to people of African, Indian, Chinese, and European descent, the island's population has spent centuries weaving together a rich cultural tapestry. Mauritian Creole — a language born from the collision of French, African languages, and others — is itself a testament to this blending. Yet even in such a diverse society, questions of identity are not always straightforward. Young Mauritians may feel simultaneously proud of their multicultural heritage and uncertain about which community they truly belong to.

Psychologists note that a strong and secure sense of cultural identity is linked to better mental health outcomes, higher self-esteem, and greater resilience in the face of adversity. Conversely, those who feel caught between cultures — neither fully accepted by one group nor the other — can experience what researchers call "cultural dissonance": a persistent feeling of not quite fitting in anywhere.

There is, however, a growing movement among young people to reframe this experience not as a source of confusion, but as a source of strength. To be fluent in multiple cultures is to possess a rare kind of empathy — the ability to see the world through more than one lens, to move between communities with understanding and grace. In a world that desperately needs bridges between peoples, this is no small gift.
