SECTION A — READING COMPREHENSION   [30 marks]

Read the passage carefully, then answer ALL the questions that follow.

Rising Waters: Climate Change and the Future of Small Island Nations

For the inhabitants of small island nations such as Mauritius, the Maldives, and Tuvalu, climate change is not a distant threat to be debated in conference rooms — it is a lived, daily reality. Scientists now warn that without dramatic reductions in global greenhouse gas emissions, many low-lying islands could face partial or total submersion by the end of this century, displacing millions of people and erasing cultures that have existed for thousands of years.

The primary driver of this crisis is the burning of fossil fuels — coal, oil, and natural gas — which releases carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases into the atmosphere. These gases act like a blanket around the Earth, trapping heat and causing global temperatures to rise. The consequences are far-reaching: glaciers melt, sea levels climb, weather patterns shift, and extreme events such as cyclones, droughts, and floods become more frequent and more severe.

For island communities, the effects are already painfully visible. Coastal erosion is swallowing beaches and farmland. Coral reefs — vital ecosystems that protect coastlines and support fisheries — are bleaching and dying as ocean temperatures rise. Freshwater supplies are becoming contaminated with saltwater as seas encroach further inland. In some Pacific islands, entire villages have already been relocated to higher ground, a process that uproots families, destroys livelihoods, and severs communities from the ancestral lands that define their identity.

Critics of strong climate action often argue that the economic cost of transitioning away from fossil fuels is too high, particularly for developing nations. However, economists increasingly warn that the cost of inaction will be far greater. The World Bank estimates that without intervention, climate change could push over 200 million people into internal migration by 2050, creating enormous social and economic pressures that no government will be able to ignore.

The good news is that solutions exist. Renewable energy technologies — solar, wind, and tidal power — have fallen dramatically in price over the past decade and are now competitive with fossil fuels in many markets. Reforestation programmes, sustainable agriculture, and improved coastal defences can all contribute to building resilience. What is required, above all else, is the political will to act before the point of no return is reached. For the people of the world's smallest island nations, that time is already running out.
