B2 — The Immune Response
PartAccepted AnswerMarks
(a) A pathogen is a microorganism (e.g. bacterium, virus) that causes disease (1). An antigen is a specific molecule (usually a protein) found on the surface of a pathogen that is recognised as foreign by the immune system and triggers an immune response (1). 2
(b) Phagocytes detect and move towards pathogens by chemotaxis (1). They engulf the pathogen by phagocytosis (changing shape to surround it), then enzymes within lysosomes are released to digest and destroy the pathogen (1). 2
(c) Memory cells (B-lymphocytes) produced during the first infection remain in the blood (1). On second infection, these memory cells recognise the same antigen and produce antibodies much more rapidly and in larger quantities, clearing the infection before serious symptoms develop. 1
B3 — The Reflex Arc
PartAccepted AnswerMarks
(a) Relay neuron (OR interneuron / connector neuron). 1
(b) In a reflex action the nerve impulse passes through the spinal cord only — it does not need to travel all the way to the brain for processing (1). This shorter pathway reduces the time taken for the response; voluntary actions require signals to be sent to and processed by the brain before a response is sent back, which takes considerably longer (1). 2
(c) Example: withdrawing your hand from a hot object (pain withdrawal reflex). Survival value: it removes the body part from danger before the brain has time to consciously process the stimulus, minimising tissue damage (1 mark for example; 1 mark for survival value). 2
B4 — Disease: Types and Prevention
PartAccepted AnswerMarks
(a)(i) Malaria — caused by a protozoan (Plasmodium). 1
(a)(ii) Tuberculosis (TB) — caused by bacteria (Mycobacterium tuberculosis). 1
(a)(iii) Influenza — caused by a virus (influenza virus). 1
(b) Any TWO of: (1) Provide clean, treated drinking water / boil water before drinking. (2) Improve sanitation — proper sewage disposal to prevent contamination of water supplies. (3) Wash hands thoroughly with soap after using the toilet and before handling food. (4) Cook food properly and avoid consuming contaminated food. (5) Oral rehydration therapy for infected individuals to prevent further spread via poor sanitation. 2
SECTION C — Extended Questions
C1 — The Nervous System [8 marks]
PartAccepted AnswerMarks
(a) Sensory neuron: carries impulses FROM a receptor (e.g. skin) TOWARDS the spinal cord / CNS; has a long dendron and short axon (1).
Relay neuron (interneuron): found within the spinal cord / brain; connects sensory neurons to motor neurons; processes and relays the impulse (1).
Motor neuron: carries impulses FROM the spinal cord / CNS TO an effector (muscle or gland); has a long myelinated axon (1). 		3
(b) When an impulse arrives at the pre-synaptic terminal (axon terminal), it triggers vesicles containing neurotransmitters to fuse with the pre-synaptic membrane (1). Neurotransmitters are released by exocytosis into the synaptic cleft / gap (1). The neurotransmitters diffuse across the cleft and bind to specific receptor sites on the post-synaptic membrane, generating a new electrical impulse in the next neuron (1). 3
(c) The central nervous system (CNS) consists of the brain and spinal cord; the peripheral nervous system (PNS) consists of all the nerves connecting the CNS to the rest of the body. (Accept any one clear structural OR functional difference.) 1
(d) Any TWO correct pairs (award 0.5 per correct pair, but as the question is [1 mark], award 1 if at least two correct): Cerebrum — conscious thought, memory, language, voluntary movement / Cerebellum — balance and coordination / Medulla oblongata — controls heart rate, breathing rate, blood pressure / Hypothalamus — regulates body temperature, hunger, thirst. 1
C2 — Disease and Immunity [7 marks]
PartAccepted AnswerMarks
(a) Active immunity: the body’s own immune system produces antibodies in response to exposure to an antigen; it is long-lasting because memory cells are produced (1). Example: recovering from an infection (natural active immunity) OR being vaccinated (artificial active immunity) (1).
Passive immunity: ready-made antibodies are introduced into the body; it is short-lived because no memory cells are produced (1). Example: antibodies passed from mother to baby via placenta or breast milk (natural passive) OR injection of antitoxin / antiserum (artificial passive) (accept one example). 		3
(b) A vaccine contains a harmless form of a pathogen (dead/weakened pathogen, antigen, or mRNA coding for an antigen) which is introduced into the body (1). The immune system responds by producing antibodies and memory B-cells specific to that antigen. If the real pathogen later infects the body, the memory cells rapidly multiply and produce large amounts of antibodies before the pathogen can cause serious illness (1). 2
(c) Within a population of bacteria there is natural genetic variation; some bacteria may have a random mutation that makes them resistant to an antibiotic (1). When antibiotics are used, non-resistant bacteria are killed while resistant ones survive and reproduce (natural selection), passing on the resistance gene to offspring — so the proportion of resistant bacteria in the population increases over time (1). 2
